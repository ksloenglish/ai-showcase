# Create an illustrated vocabulary revision video

## What this makes

A vocabulary revision video in which students use pictures and first-letter clues to guess phrases and words, then check the answers and pronunciation. It also includes a ready-to-copy YouTube title and description; if your assistant cannot make videos, it can prepare a complete video plan and script instead.

## What you need

- A vocabulary list in Word, PDF, Excel or a pasted table. Include each full headword, part of speech, English definition, Traditional Chinese meaning and example sentence.
- The year group, exact video title, school or channel name, tutor name and preferred voice.
- Optional reference pictures, a transparent school logo, a tutor picture and any existing introduction, transition, encouragement or ending clips you want to reuse. Upload these if you want the new video to match an existing one.

## How to use it

1. Prepare the material listed above. Include only the unit or items you want to revise.
2. Open your chosen AI assistant and attach or paste the material.
3. Copy the whole prompt below. Change only the words in `[SQUARE BRACKETS]` if needed. British English and Traditional Chinese are the suggested defaults.
4. Send the prompt. Respond to the requests to approve the spoken forms, sample pictures and complete image set. Read through and watch the result before giving it to students.

## Copy-and-paste prompt

```text
You are helping me create an illustrated English vocabulary revision video for [YEAR GROUP] students.

Video title: [EXACT VIDEO TITLE]
School or channel name: [SCHOOL OR CHANNEL NAME]
Virtual tutor name: [TUTOR NAME]
Preferred voice or supplied voice reference: [PREFERRED VOICE OR VOICE REFERENCE]
English variety: [British English]
Language for the translated meanings: [Traditional Chinese]

### Materials
I have attached or pasted the vocabulary list and any logo, tutor picture, example artwork, voice reference or reusable clips I want you to use.

Use only information you can read from these materials for the vocabulary content. Do not invent, alter or silently omit headwords, definitions, meanings or example sentences. If a required field is missing or seems incorrect, flag it and ask whether I will supply it or want a draft for approval. Do not quietly rewrite an approved source item, add an article or change its spelling.

### Task
Create a professional but appealing revision video for secondary students. Students should see an illustration, guess the vocabulary using first letters and an example-sentence clue, say the answer during a three-second countdown, then check the spoken answer and meaning. Put phrases first, followed by words. Use a friendly, consistent virtual tutor and a different memorable original fantasy-creature partner in each illustration.

Before starting, explain briefly whether you can actually create and inspect images, generate and listen to narration, and assemble an MP4. If any part is unavailable, say so and offer a complete editable video plan and script instead. Do not describe image instructions as finished pictures or a storyboard as a finished video.

### Requirements

1. Use all the supplied items exactly once.
Separate them into Part 1: Phrases and Part 2: Words. Preserve the source order within each part. Do not add items to reach a round number. Omit an empty part rather than inventing its contents. Use my exact title and the actual number of items in all counters and completion messages.

2. Agree the display and spoken forms before recording item narration.
Show one complete review table containing each full headword, the proposed quiz answer, the exact words the tutor will say, the main first-letter blanks and the example-sentence clue. Wait for my explicit approval before generating any item audio.

Remove trailing dictionary placeholders such as sb, sth, sb/sth and a trailing do sth from the quiz answer and speech, while retaining necessary words such as to. Keep the full original headword on the meaning card and in the YouTube vocabulary list. For placeholders inside a phrase, apostrophes, brackets, slashes or other ambiguous forms, give concrete options and ask rather than guessing. Do not omit an item without permission.

If I approve a visual ellipsis, keep its dots visible during the countdown and answer reveal, but do not speak the dots or placeholder. Keep a hyphenated word as one word, preserve the hyphen and show an initial for each component. Confirm unusual cases in the review table; do not assume that an earlier decision about another phrase applies automatically.

3. Make the first-letter activity accurate.
Under “What's the phrase?” or “What's the word?”, show the initial of each word followed by one blank per missing letter. Leave single-letter words visible. Use natural spaces between words and keep everything readable without cutting off the row.

Below that row, show the supplied example sentence with only the target expression blanked out. Each sentence-clue blank must have the same length, regardless of the word's length, and retain its first letter and original capitalisation. Blank the target's actual inflected form in the sentence, not a substituted dictionary form. Leave one-letter words visible. For a phrase beginning with be, leave the sentence's form of be fully visible.

For a target split by other words, blank only the relevant target words and leave the intervening words visible. Check every clue and highlight; do not leave a supposed guessing sentence with no target blank.

4. Plan meaningful, original illustrations.
Read both the definition and example sentence before designing each scene. Depict that meaning in that context, not another sense of the word. Keep the person doing or experiencing the action consistent with the sentence.

Every picture must contain a clearly readable human participant and a prominent, adorable, ORIGINAL animal-inspired fantasy creature. The creature must help explain the meaning through a shared activity, social role, useful action or strong relevant emotional response. It must not be a tiny decorative pet or a smiling observer added to a corner. The human need not be larger than the creature, but both the human's role and the creature's face and action must be clear.

Use vibrant anime-style artwork, confident outlines, smooth cel shading, expressive faces and distinctive ears, tails and markings. Avoid generic plush baby animals, abstract blobs, realistic scaly lizards and aggressive designs. Do not copy existing franchise characters, recognisable lookalikes, branded accessories or creature-catching devices.

Give each image a genuinely different creature identity. Vary body shape, proportions, ears, tails, markings and fantasy features—not just colour or clothing. For related meanings, vary the meaningful activity, posture, props or viewpoint as well. For example, do not reuse essentially the same standing exercise scene for several exercise-related items.

Keep generated scene artwork free of lettering, numbers, logos, arrows, floating symbols, thought or speech bubbles, game statistics and card frames. Add educational text separately in the video layout. Keep serious situations dignified rather than using unrelated cute comedy.

5. Check anatomy, devices and physical details before showing images to me.
Inspect actual images at a useful size, not just their written descriptions. Trace every visible human hand to a plausible arm. Account for the creature's intended limbs, including raised or naturally hidden ones; do not count a tail as a missing leg or force a bird to have a mammal's body plan.

Birds should have one coherent beak and mouth, not a separate human-like mouth below the bill. Check that faces are natural within the chosen style. If an edited face remains strange or I reject the patched character, stop making repeated small patches and propose regenerating the whole scene with a new original character.

Check clothing against the actual waterline. For a person wading, use suitable clothing or dry rolled-up cuffs above the water with bare lower legs below; do not accidentally submerge trouser-covered legs without a sensible reason.

Keep ordinary devices physically ordinary. Laptop screens face their users and connect by a plausible hinge to the keyboard; the outside lid must not become a second screen. Phone and tablet backs must not have accidental displays. Conventional screenless smart speakers should not acquire invented picture screens. Check grips, support, scale and contact with furniture.

Keep faces, important limbs, key props and the creature's distinctive shape safely inside the picture at both the start and the maximum slight zoom, approximately six per cent. Judge readability at the size the picture will occupy in the video, not only in the large original image.

6. Obtain three separate kinds of approval.
First, obtain approval of the complete spoken-form review table before generating item audio.

Second, create three representative pictures from the real vocabulary list: a shared-action scene, an emotional or contextual scene, and a prop or device scene if relevant. If the list has fewer than three items, show all of them. Show these samples inside a proposed countdown/reveal layout and wait for style approval before generating the remaining images.

Third, after generating and checking the complete set, show ALL images—including the samples—in a readable numbered gallery with a way to open each full-size image. Wait for my explicit approval of the complete image set before assembling the video. Style approval does not approve images I have not seen; approval of the spoken list does not approve images either. Do not treat silence as approval. One approval of the complete gallery is enough; do not ask for a separate reply for every image.

If I approve some images and reject others, keep the approved versions unchanged. Correct only the rejected images, show their replacements and wait for approval. Any later change to an accepted image requires a fresh preview and approval. Prepare approved audio independently if helpful, but do not assemble the final video while image approval is outstanding.

7. Keep the tutor and visual identity consistent.
Use the supplied tutor reference. If none is supplied, propose a friendly young female anime tutor with shoulder-length brown hair, a gold star hair clip, navy blazer, white shirt and coral bow for my approval. Keep the same face, outfit, proportions and name throughout.

Use a navy, gold and coral theme, clear English and Chinese fonts, and generous spacing. Keep an original transparent logo unaltered apart from proportional resizing; never redraw it or add an opaque box, glow or backing behind it.

Start with the logo centred, then move it upward to join the title as one coherent group. When the tutor enters on the right, move the title group into a left-aligned column, leaving breathing space and placing the tutor's name and “Your Virtual English Tutor” beside her. Do not shrink the logo and fly it into the upper-left corner during the opening. A small logo may appear in the item-screen header.

Keep the tutor's welcome, transition, encouragement and goodbye generic enough to reuse with later units. Show the particular unit title on screen rather than recording a new unit-specific welcome. Reuse any supplied accepted clips, music and tutor assets instead of remaking them unnecessarily.

8. Use a clear per-item sequence.
Make the video 16:9, preferably 1920 × 1080. Allow about 8.6 seconds per item plus the welcome, part cards, permitted encouragements and ending; determine the total length from the actual list.

On each item screen, show the title and an “ITEM n / total” counter with progress. Place the main illustration prominently on the left. Use the upper-right area for a three-second film-style countdown, followed by the meaning card. Place a small round tutor portrait beneath it, with “Say it out loud!” during guessing and a clear speaking indicator only while the answer is spoken. Do not generate a separate animated tutor clip for every vocabulary item.

Use the lower panel for the question, first-letter blanks and sentence clue. Run the 3–2–1 countdown with gentle beeps or projector-style effects. When time is up, reveal the approved answer, play the tutor's answer once and replace the clue with the complete supplied sentence, highlighting exactly the target expression, including its inflected form.

The meaning card must show the full headword first, a compact part-of-speech label without a full stop, the translated meaning and the English definition. Keep the complete highlighted example sentence readable in the lower panel. Do not replace the full headword with its shortened spoken form everywhere.

9. Make narration complete, natural and consistent.
Use the preferred available voice or supplied voice reference. If it is unavailable, tell me and obtain approval before substituting another voice. Use a youthful, cheerful, natural tutor voice—not exaggerated cartoon delivery or artificial pitch lifting. State any limitations in matching the reference or requested accent.

Record or prepare each vocabulary answer separately to avoid list-like, rising intonation. Say exactly the approved lexical form, including any article that genuinely belongs to it, but not dictionary placeholders, ellipses, spelling symbols, carrier sentences or extra words. Every answer should end with a calm falling statement tone.

Check the actual syllable stress, weak vowels, all function words and final consonants. For example, concern needs second-syllable stress; boost needs a complete /s/ followed by a natural final /t/, not a missing consonant or an extra “uh”. Do not accept a word merely because speech recognition spells it correctly. A transcript cannot establish correct accent, stress or a quiet unvoiced consonant.

If an answer is unclear, make a better same-voice recording and check it again. If a carrier sentence or repeated reading helps, remove everything except the complete intended answer, leaving enough space to preserve the initial and final sounds. Do not put pronunciation respellings on screen. Match the item volume and retain the voice's natural character; do not silently change the whole voice to repair one word.

10. Use encouragement without disrupting the activity.
After each fifth item within a part, add a short tutor encouragement only if at least three items remain in that same part. Do not count items in the next part. Never leave one or two items isolated between an encouragement and the part transition. Reserve an “Almost there!” message for the last permitted encouragement.

Show a short speech bubble and “n of total done” progress for that part. Fit every word and punctuation mark inside a padded safe area at a readable size. Do not solve overflow by cropping, hiding words or adding an ellipsis. Check all planned encouragement messages, including their entry, largest animation size, hold and exit.

11. Keep motion and sound comfortable.
Where the tutor moves, gestures must match the spoken words. A goodbye wave belongs with the goodbye, not before it. Avoid extra hands, sudden arm snaps, changing body size, jumpy positioning and clips cut in the middle of a gesture. Do not speed up or distort clean speech to fit an animation.

Use soft upbeat music, quieter beneath narration, with restrained logo, transition, countdown and reveal effects. Keep answers equally clear and loud. End with a brief encouraging conclusion, then only the logo and “Thanks for revising with [TUTOR NAME]!” centred vertically. Hold briefly, fade smoothly to black and leave about one second of silent black at the end.

12. Make corrections without disturbing accepted work.
Keep the previous version before editing. For an image-only correction, change only the agreed image and its scene; preserve approved wording, item order, timing, narration, music and other images. Show the replacement for approval.

For a named pronunciation correction, replace only the named answers in the same voice, preserving the other words, images and music as closely as the available tools allow. Update the affected speaking indicator if needed and check that the new sound is not cut off or masked by effects. Do not start an unrequested whole-video pronunciation audit or change the voice. If exact preservation cannot be checked, say so rather than claiming it.

### Output
If you can create the media, provide:
- The completed 16:9 MP4.
- The numbered approved illustration files and the complete image-review gallery.
- The approved spoken-form table.
- A Markdown file containing separate ready-to-copy YouTube title and description sections.
- A short note identifying any genuine unresolved issue; do not hide it in a claim that everything passed.

Use the chosen English variety for new explanatory text. Preserve approved vocabulary-source wording unless I authorise a change.

For the YouTube title, use my exact video title followed by “| Vocabulary Revision with [TUTOR NAME] | [SCHOOL OR CHANNEL NAME]”. If it exceeds 100 characters, propose a shorter title for approval instead of silently changing mine.

The description must include a friendly introduction, the intended learner group, how to guess and say answers during the countdown, what students see after the reveal, and its use before a dictation, quiz or test. Include chapters for Welcome, Phrases, Words and Wrap-up when present, using the actual final video times—not estimated times copied from another video. Include the full original headwords, placeholders included, in their actual part order with correct counts. Add revision tips: say answers aloud, pause for more time, watch again and make your own sentences. Finish with encouragement and suitable school, year-group and English-vocabulary hashtags.

Keep YouTube copy about the learning activity. Do not include production notes, version numbers, model names, franchise branding or claims about how the material was generated. Do not claim a fully British accent or any other quality you have not verified. Provide copy only; do not upload or publish anything without a separate request. If there is no finished video, label chapter timings as a plan to be confirmed, not final timestamps.

If you cannot make the requested media, provide one complete editable Markdown plan instead. Include a scene-by-scene table with planned times, exact on-screen text, exact narration, sentence clues, complete answers/meaning cards, human–creature illustration descriptions, tutor appearances and permitted encouragement positions. Include a list of pictures and audio needed, the approval steps, and draft YouTube copy. Clearly identify what still needs creating or checking. If you cannot generate pictures, provide image descriptions without pretending they are approved images.

### Check before delivery
Check that:
- Every supplied item appears exactly once, phrases before words, in source order within each part, with accurate counts and no invented content.
- Spoken forms were approved before item narration, three real-item samples were approved before the remaining images, and ALL actual images were approved before assembly.
- Clues, blanks, hyphens, visible ellipses, inflections and target highlights are correct; full headwords and source sentences remain intact.
- Each illustration matches the sentence meaning and has a clear human plus a prominent, meaningful, distinct original creature—not a repeated or decorative pet.
- Every face, beak, limb, device and waterline makes sense, and important details remain visible at the actual video size and maximum zoom.
- Every answer is complete and natural, with correct stress, clear function words, audible final consonants, a falling ending and no extra words. Listen again in the final music-and-effects mix.
- If you use a transcript, inspect the actual transcript rather than repeated display snippets. Treat transcription errors as uncertain and check the sound; do not use corrected spelling as proof of correct pronunciation.
- Every encouragement has at least three following items in its own part, and all its text remains readable throughout its animation.
- The opening, title, logo, tutor, sentence text, meaning cards, progress, gestures and final fade are correct in the finished video, not just in previews. The last black frame is silent.
- The video plays through without missing scenes, damaged audio or abrupt cuts, and the YouTube title, lists, counts and final chapter times match it.
- Any selective revision preserves accepted material outside the agreed changes, and any check you cannot perform is explicitly identified.

If you cannot read an attachment, create a requested file, listen to the audio, inspect the images or verify the finished layout, say so before proceeding. Offer the closest useful text or video-plan version and give me a short list of checks to do myself. Do not claim to have completed a capability you do not have.

Ask one focused clarification question only if an essential input is missing. Otherwise, proceed with the first planning and approval stage. The required approval pauses are separate from clarification questions: do not skip them or run ahead to the final video.
```

## Before you use the result with students

- Compare the item list, meanings and example sentences with your source. Check the clues, reveals and word/phrase counts.
- Review every picture, opening and meaning card. Look closely at faces, limbs, devices, waterlines and any text that may be cut off.
- Listen to the whole video, especially words you asked to correct. Check syllable stress, final consonants and any unwanted extra words.
- Check encouragement positions and final chapter times. Do not rely only on an automatic “passed” message.

## If your AI assistant cannot make the file you want

Ask for the complete Markdown video plan and script described in the prompt. You can use these with a video editor and the picture or narration tools available to you; timings remain provisional until you have assembled and checked the video.

## What you can change

Change the bracketed year group, exact title, school or channel name, tutor name, preferred voice, English variety and meaning language. Upload your own pictures and reusable clips if you want a different visual identity.

Keep the source-wording checks, three approval stages, meaningful human–creature illustrations, minimum-three encouragement rule and final pronunciation checks: these protect the accuracy and usefulness of the activity.
